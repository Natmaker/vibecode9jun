Abstract

What makes a startup
actually succeed?

The rapid development of technology and the informatisation of society have produced the phenomenon of startup companies — a force that marked the economy at the end of the 20th and the beginning of the 21st century. Unlike traditional small and medium-sized enterprises, startups are built on innovation and technological development, typically around a single, highly differentiated product or service that disrupts or advances a market.

This research sets out to determine the success factors specific to this form of entrepreneurship, alongside the innovative approaches to organisation, management, and financing that define it. The characteristic life cycle of a startup company is identified and mapped.

Through a case study and a review of key scientific literature, the research demonstrates the close connection between the psychological characteristics of entrepreneurs and their likelihood of success — as well as the critical importance of the business ecosystem and support organisations in which a startup operates.

The paper combines a theoretical overview of startup history and evolution with an empirical case study of a successful, newly founded IT startup with an innovative product.

Research Hypotheses

Four propositions,
all confirmed.

Based on a structured review of entrepreneurship literature and an empirical case study, four working hypotheses were defined at the outset of the research. Each was tested through theory and practice, and each was confirmed.

H1

Entrepreneur

The personal characteristics of the entrepreneur are crucial for the creation of a new venture.

Psychological, sociological, and demographic traits — risk propensity, creativity, temperament, and prior experience — fundamentally shape an entrepreneur's capacity to recognise opportunity and build something lasting. Bolton and Thompson's FACETS framework (Focus, Advantage, Creativity, Ego, Team, Society) was central to this analysis.

H2

Innovation

The development of an innovative product or service is of critical importance for the growth and success of startup companies.

Research consistently demonstrates a strong correlation between innovative products and market success. Startups that create genuinely differentiated offerings — rather than incremental improvements to existing ones — gain structural competitive advantages that are difficult to replicate, particularly in their early market phase.

H3

Organisation

The success of a startup is determined by creating a creative organisation, entrepreneurial orientation, and constant introduction of innovative changes.

Entrepreneurial orientation — a company's tendency to act innovatively, take risks, and pursue market opportunities proactively — was shown to positively affect both financial and non-financial performance. A flat structure, a culture of creative brainstorming, and synergistic teamwork are hallmarks of startups that sustain their initial success.

H4

Ecosystem

Entrepreneurial support organisations are a key factor in helping startups create successful organisations.

80% of startups outside incubator systems fail within five years, compared to an 87% survival rate for those within them. Incubators, accelerators, and networks provide not only subsidised space but advisory, legal, educational, and logistical support during the most vulnerable phases of a company's development.

Research Themes

Six areas of
investigation.

◈

History & Evolution

From Silicon Valley's "traitorous eight" in 1957 through the dot-com bubble and the lean startup revolution — tracing how the concept of the startup emerged and matured.

◈

Financing Sources

Bootstrapping, FFF capital, business angels, venture capital funds, and bank financing — each source carries distinct trade-offs for control, risk, and growth potential.

◈

The Startup Ecosystem

Universities, incubators, accelerators, investors, and service providers form a dynamic open system in which each element plays a specific role at different phases of a startup's life.

◈

Entrepreneurial Potential

Bolton and Thompson's BTEI framework and the FACETS model provide structured tools for identifying individuals with genuine entrepreneurial capacity — separate from general business skill.

◈

Innovation Theory

From Schumpeter's creative destruction to Drucker's innovation-as-tool and the lean startup methodology — analysing how innovation functions as a competitive strategy.

◈

Measuring Success

Financial indicators (profitability, liquidity, leverage) and non-financial ones (market share, customer satisfaction, employee engagement) together form a complete picture of startup performance.

Empirical Research

Case Study: Maracom Software

Maracom Software was founded in 2016 by Ivan Marković, a 22-year-old programmer and economics student, after he identified a significant gap in the Croatian market: no adequate software solution existed for managing the day-to-day operations of driving schools.

Working from personal savings — with a computer, programming knowledge, and an idea as his only initial capital — Marković developed a prototype, tested it with a real client under controlled conditions, iterated based on feedback, and launched commercially within one year. The product required almost no upfront investment beyond intellectual effort.

Upon founding the company, Marković joined the PISAK entrepreneurial incubator in Sisak — the first privately initiated incubator in Croatia — where access to affordable premises, administrative support, mentoring, and a network of fellow entrepreneurs provided the stability needed to scale rapidly.

The resulting software system managed instructor scheduling, candidate tracking, exam results, GPS vehicle monitoring, invoice generation, and statistical reporting across four levels of user authorisation. A companion exam-preparation module led to an 85% increase in candidate pass rates across partner driving schools.

By April 2018, Maracom held a 100% market share in its core segment in Croatia, with 400 active users and a retained profit trajectory pointing towards 2 million kuna in annual revenue before the end of that year.

Conclusions

What the research
confirmed.

All four hypotheses were confirmed through the theoretical review and the case study evidence. The findings point to a consistent conclusion: startup success is not a product of any single factor, but of an interlocking system in which entrepreneurial character, innovative product development, organisational culture, and ecosystem support all operate together.

Startups are not simply smaller versions of established companies. They are temporary organisations searching for a scalable and repeatable business model — and the forces that determine whether they find it or fail are specific, identifiable, and to some extent manageable.

  • H1 Confirmed: An entrepreneur's personal traits — creativity, risk tolerance, temperament, and drive — are decisive in the creation and early development of a new venture. These qualities cannot be fully taught; they must be present at the outset.
  • H2 Confirmed: Innovation is the primary differentiator. Startups that bring genuinely new products to market — rather than competing on price or incremental improvement — gain structural advantages that are difficult for incumbents to overcome quickly.
  • H3 Confirmed: Entrepreneurial orientation — the combination of innovativeness, proactivity, and calculated risk-taking at every level of the organisation — is directly correlated with sustained performance. Creative culture is a strategic asset, not a soft benefit.
  • H4 Confirmed: Entrepreneurial incubators and support organisations significantly increase the probability of startup survival and success. Their value extends well beyond subsidised rent — mentoring, networking, and access to institutional knowledge are equally important.
